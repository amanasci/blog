There is something I used to find deeply frustrating about the way we were taught as children.

Why did we have to memorize so much?

Definitions, equations, historical dates, chemical reactions, entire paragraphs from textbooks. We were expected to learn them, reproduce them in an examination, and then, in many cases, promptly forget them. I could never quite understand the point of this exercise. If I understood the underlying concept, why should it matter whether I could recall the exact definition? Why should I spend hours memorizing something when I could simply look it up whenever I needed it?

I suspect many of us have had some version of this thought. We grow up associating memorization with rote learning, rote learning with bad education, and bad education with the kind of examinations that reward remembering what the teacher said rather than understanding why it was true.

And there is a reasonable criticism buried in all of this. Memorizing an equation does not mean you understand it. Being able to reproduce a proof does not mean you could construct it yourself. You can memorize the definition of a derivative and still have very little intuition about what a derivative actually represents.

For a long time, I think I took this criticism a step further than I should have. I began to treat memorization itself as something redundant, almost as if it were an unfortunate by-product of the way we teach rather than an important part of learning.

Lately, however, I've started reconsidering this.

The more I think about learning difficult subjects, the more I wonder whether I had the relationship between memory and understanding backward. Perhaps memorization isn't merely something we do before we can begin to understand. Perhaps, under the right conditions, it is one of the things that makes deeper understanding possible in the first place.

This is not an argument for bringing back the kind of education where students mechanically reproduce pages of a textbook. It is an attempt to understand a more interesting question: what does it actually mean to know something, and why does being able to recall it matter?

## The strange difference between recognizing something and knowing it

Consider a simple experiment.

Suppose I give you a textbook on classical mechanics and ask you to read a chapter on Newton's laws. You read it carefully. You follow the examples, understand the derivations, and feel reasonably confident that you have grasped the material.

Now close the book.

Can you explain Newton's second law without looking at your notes? Can you derive the equations of motion for a simple system? Can you explain when $F=ma$ is an appropriate description, and what assumptions lie behind its use? Can you connect it to a problem you have never seen before?

These are different questions, and the differences between them are revealing.

While reading, the information is available to you. The equations are right there. The explanations are right there. You can follow the author's reasoning as it unfolds, and each sentence provides cues for understanding the next one.

But when the book closes, those cues disappear.

You are now being asked to reconstruct something from memory.

And this is where an uncomfortable gap often appears. Something that seemed perfectly understandable a few minutes ago becomes surprisingly difficult to explain. You remember the general idea but cannot reproduce the derivation. You recognize the equation when you see it but cannot recall it independently. You understand the author's explanation but struggle to construct an explanation of your own.

I have increasingly come to think that this gap is important.

**Recognizing information when it is presented to you is not the same as being able to retrieve and use it independently.**

Psychologists distinguish between different aspects of memory and learning. Recognition, recall, retention, and the ability to apply knowledge are related, but they are not interchangeable.

And because we often study by reading, watching lectures, or following worked examples, we can become very good at recognizing information without becoming equally good at retrieving it.

This creates a peculiar illusion. We feel as though we have learned something because it looks familiar. But familiarity is not necessarily evidence of durable learning.

The real test comes when we have to reconstruct the idea ourselves.

## What happens when you try to remember?

In 2006, psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke published a paper in *Psychological Science* titled [*Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x).

The paper examined something that seems almost counterintuitive when we first encounter it: testing yourself on material can improve your ability to remember it later.

In their experiments, participants studied prose passages. Some repeatedly studied the material, while others practised recalling it from memory. The researchers then tested retention after different delays.

Repeated study could be advantageous when the test came soon after learning. But when retention was measured after a longer delay, retrieval practice often produced better results.

Think about what this means.

We generally imagine studying as the process of putting information into our heads. A test then measures how much of that information remains.

But the experiments suggest that retrieving information is not merely a way of measuring learning. **Retrieval itself can change learning.**

A subsequent paper by Jeffrey Karpicke and Roediger, [*The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408), published in *Science* in 2008, further explored the importance of retrieval practice for long-term retention.

This is sometimes called the *testing effect* or *retrieval practice effect*.

The terminology is slightly unfortunate because it makes the technique sound as though it belongs primarily in an examination hall. But you do not need a formal test. You can close a book and explain a concept to yourself. You can derive an equation on a blank sheet of paper. You can try to reconstruct the central argument of a research paper before looking at it again.

All of these can provide opportunities to practise retrieving knowledge.

There is an important qualification here. Not every attempt to remember will necessarily improve learning. Difficulty alone is not the objective. If you cannot recall something, feedback and restudy may be necessary. The benefit depends on what you retrieve, how successfully you do it, and how you respond to errors.

Still, the basic finding changes how I think about studying.

Perhaps the question should not be only how much time we spend exposing ourselves to information. We should also ask how often we practise bringing that information back into our minds.

### An experiment you can try

The next time you finish reading a technical section, resist the temptation to immediately highlight more sentences or reread the same pages.

Close the material and ask yourself three questions:

1. What was the central idea?
2. Why does it work?
3. Could I reconstruct the argument without looking at the source?

Then reopen the material and check what you missed.

You may discover that your understanding was more fragile than you thought. That is not necessarily a failure. It is useful information about what you have and have not learned.

And the act of trying to retrieve the material gives you another opportunity to strengthen it.

## But why should remembering help us understand?

This is where I think the question becomes more interesting.

It is one thing to show that retrieval practice improves retention. It is another to explain why having knowledge readily available might make someone a better learner or a better thinker.

Consider learning Maxwell's equations.

At first, you might need to remember what a divergence is, what a curl represents, how to interpret a vector field, and what the symbols in the equations mean. You might have to stop repeatedly to look up definitions, check mathematical identities, and reconstruct elementary results.

Now imagine that you have worked with these concepts for years.

You no longer have to consciously reconstruct every elementary definition whenever you encounter a new problem. You can focus on the unfamiliar part: the boundary conditions, the physical interpretation, the approximation being made, or the mathematical structure of a new derivation.

The difference is not that the second person has magically acquired a larger brain. It is that they have built a body of knowledge that supports the task they are trying to perform.

This brings us to working memory.

Working memory refers, broadly, to the limited capacity we have for temporarily maintaining and manipulating information while performing a cognitive task. It is involved in reasoning, calculation, comprehension, and problem-solving.

When solving a difficult problem, we need to keep track of intermediate results, remember the conditions of the problem, manipulate symbols, and decide what to do next. All of these activities compete for cognitive resources.

If every elementary operation requires conscious reconstruction, the task becomes considerably more demanding.

But familiar knowledge can be retrieved and used with much less effort than completely unfamiliar material. With practice, some operations become increasingly automatic, while larger patterns become easier to recognize.

This is one reason expertise can look so different from novice performance.

A novice and an expert might be looking at the same equation. The novice sees symbols and a sequence of operations. The expert may see a familiar physical structure, a symmetry, a limiting case, or an opportunity to simplify the problem.

The expert is not necessarily performing more elaborate reasoning at every step. Sometimes the expert can reason more effectively because so much of the underlying structure is already available.

In their 1995 paper [*Long-Term Working Memory*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.2.211), K. Anders Ericsson and Walter Kintsch developed a framework for understanding how skilled individuals use long-term memory to support complex cognitive activities.

Their work offers one way to think about how experience can change the effective resources available for reasoning. Skilled performance can involve learned structures that make relevant information accessible during ongoing thought.

This does not mean that long-term memory literally eliminates the limits of working memory. Nor does memorizing a large number of facts automatically make someone an expert.

The more modest and useful conclusion is that knowledge changes the nature of the task.

When you know enough about a subject, you do not have to treat every component as a separate, unfamiliar problem. You can recognize patterns, retrieve relevant concepts, and use what you already know to make sense of what you do not yet know.

And that, I suspect, is one of the reasons learning becomes easier when we have a solid foundation.

## Knowledge is cumulative, but not in the way I once imagined

Think about learning a subject such as machine learning.

Suppose you are trying to understand transformers for the first time. You encounter attention mechanisms, matrix multiplication, probability distributions, optimization, loss functions, and gradient descent.

If you already understand linear algebra and probability, some of these ideas are familiar. You can connect attention to matrix operations, interpret the softmax function, and understand why an objective is being optimized.

You still have to learn something new, but you do not have to build the entire conceptual foundation from scratch.

Now imagine approaching the same material without those foundations. You may encounter dozens of unfamiliar terms, each requiring its own explanation. You must learn the terminology, understand the operations, and then combine them into a larger picture.

The difference is substantial.

This is one reason prior knowledge matters so much in learning. What you already know influences how you interpret new information, how you connect concepts, and how much of a new explanation you can follow.

The National Academies' [*How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures*](https://doi.org/10.17226/24783), published in 2018, discusses how existing knowledge shapes learning and how learners integrate new information with what they already know.

There is also a relevant review by Brod, Werkle-Bergner, and Shing, [*The Influence of Prior Knowledge on Memory: A Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience Perspective*](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2013.00139), which explores how prior knowledge affects the encoding, consolidation, and retrieval of information.

Together, these perspectives suggest that learning is not simply a matter of accumulating isolated facts.

Knowledge is connected. A new concept becomes easier to understand when it can be related to concepts you already know. The more useful connections you have, the more ways you may have to interpret and retrieve the new information.

But this raises an important distinction.

**Accumulating information is not the same as building a useful knowledge structure.**

You could memorize hundreds of disconnected facts about physics without developing much ability to solve physics problems. Conversely, a relatively small number of well-understood principles, connected to examples and applications, can support a surprising amount of reasoning.

The goal is not to remember everything.

The goal is to retain the things that help you understand other things.

### Why foundations matter

Consider the derivative.

You could memorize the power rule, the product rule, and the chain rule. These are useful results, and remembering them makes calculations easier.

But a deeper understanding involves knowing how the derivative relates to rates of change, local linear approximations, and the behaviour of functions.

Once these ideas are connected, a new problem is no longer just an invitation to select a memorized formula. You can reason about what a derivative represents, decide whether differentiation is useful, and interpret the result.

Memorization supports this process when it helps you retain the mathematical tools and conceptual relationships you need. Understanding organizes those tools into a coherent structure.

Neither process completely replaces the other.

## The problem with treating memory and understanding as opposites

I think we sometimes make a category error when we criticize memorization in education.

We observe that rote memorization can produce shallow learning, and conclude that memorization itself must be a shallow activity.

But the two are not equivalent.

Consider three students learning the same theorem.

The first memorizes its statement and reproduces it during an examination. They may not understand why it is true or recognize when it can be applied.

The second understands a teacher's explanation of the theorem but rarely attempts to recall it independently. They can follow the proof when it is presented but struggle to reconstruct it later.

The third studies the theorem, tries to reproduce the proof from memory, checks the gaps in their reasoning, and applies the result to different problems.

All three students have interacted with the same material. Yet they have engaged in very different kinds of learning.

The first has practised reproduction. The second has developed some conceptual understanding but may have fragile access to the knowledge. The third has combined understanding, retrieval, feedback, and application.

This is the distinction that matters.

Memorization without understanding can be brittle. Understanding without durable retention can be difficult to use. Learning becomes more powerful when we combine conceptual understanding with the ability to retrieve and apply what we know.

A useful review of this broader question is Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 paper, [*Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266).

The authors evaluated ten commonly used learning techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice received particularly strong recommendations because their benefits were supported across a range of learning conditions. Rereading and highlighting, while not entirely useless, were less reliable when used as stand-alone strategies.

One implication is that we should be careful about equating the feeling of understanding something with having learned it well.

Rereading can make information feel familiar. Following a derivation can make it seem obvious. But learning also requires being able to reconstruct, explain, and use the material when the original explanation is no longer in front of you.

The distinction is subtle but consequential.

The question is not whether we should memorize instead of understanding.

It is whether we can develop understanding that survives beyond the moment in which the information is presented to us.

## There is still a reason to be suspicious of memorization

At this point, it would be easy to swing too far in the opposite direction.

If memory is so important, should we simply start memorizing more? Should we build flashcards for every equation, definition, and paragraph? Should we try to retain as much information as possible?

I don't think so.

For one thing, our memory is not a perfect recording device. Remembering is reconstructive, and recall can be incomplete or distorted. We can remember something incorrectly and become increasingly confident in the wrong answer if we never check it.

Prior knowledge can also be inaccurate. An existing misconception may interfere with learning something new. And the ability to recall a fact does not guarantee that we understand its meaning or know how to apply it.

There is another problem: time is limited.

If I spend an entire afternoon memorizing details from a paper that I could easily retrieve when needed, I may be sacrificing time that would be better spent understanding the paper's argument, reproducing its experiments, or comparing its assumptions with those of another approach.

Not all knowledge deserves equal effort.

Some things are useful precisely because we can retrieve them quickly: basic definitions, mathematical identities, central principles, and concepts that repeatedly appear in our work. Other details are better left to reference materials.

The distinction depends on the task.

For a physicist, it may be valuable to know certain mathematical identities and physical principles well enough to use them without repeatedly consulting a textbook. That does not mean the physicist needs to memorize every constant, every specialized integral, or every result in every paper.

For a machine learning researcher, it may be useful to remember the assumptions behind an optimization method, the meaning of an objective function, or the basic structure of an architecture. It is not necessarily useful to memorize every hyperparameter setting in a paper they read once.

A reasonable principle is this:

**Remember what you need to think with. Look up what you only occasionally need to know.**

Even this is not an absolute rule. What is worth remembering depends on your goals, the frequency with which you use a concept, the consequences of getting it wrong, and how easily you can retrieve a reliable reference.

But it is a better starting point than either memorizing everything or dismissing memorization entirely.

## Perhaps the real skill is learning how to remember

Once we recognize the value of retrieval, the next question is practical: how should we study?

I used to think that studying meant spending more time with the material. Read the chapter again. Watch another lecture. Go through the notes one more time.

These activities can be useful, particularly when we are first trying to understand something. But they can also become a comfortable loop in which we repeatedly encounter the same information without testing whether we can retrieve it.

A different approach is to alternate between studying and remembering.

Suppose I am learning Bayesian inference.

I first try to understand the basic idea. I work through an example, examine the assumptions, and make sure I understand what the prior, likelihood, and posterior represent.

Then I close the material.

Can I explain Bayes' theorem from memory? Can I derive it from the definition of conditional probability? Can I explain why the normalizing constant appears? Can I solve a simple example without copying the steps from the textbook?

If I cannot, I return to the source, identify what I missed, and try again.

Later, I revisit the concept after some time has passed. I try to recall it again rather than immediately rereading the explanation.

Finally, I use it in a new context. Perhaps I examine a Bayesian model, compare two priors, or ask how the same reasoning appears in another statistical problem.

This approach combines several learning strategies:

- **Retrieval practice:** trying to bring knowledge to mind without immediately consulting the source.
- **Spaced practice:** revisiting material across multiple sessions rather than concentrating all study into one sitting.
- **Feedback:** checking whether the retrieved information is correct and correcting errors.
- **Application:** using the knowledge to solve problems or explain unfamiliar situations.

These strategies address different parts of the learning process. Retrieval helps practise access to knowledge; spacing helps support retention over time; feedback helps correct errors; and application helps us learn when and how to use what we know.

None is a magical solution. The appropriate amount of practice depends on the material and the learner, and difficult material often requires a mixture of explanation, worked examples, problem-solving, and retrieval.

But this approach changes the purpose of studying.

Instead of asking, "How many times have I read this?", we can ask, "What can I reconstruct without looking, and what can I do with it?"

That is a much more revealing question.

## From remembering facts to building intuition

There is one final distinction I want to make.

I do not think the ultimate goal of learning is to become a person who can recall enormous amounts of information. A database can store facts. A search engine can retrieve them. Neither ability, by itself, amounts to understanding.

What interests me is the possibility that remembered knowledge becomes something more than a collection of facts.

Consider someone who has spent years studying a mathematical subject. They encounter a new result and immediately notice that it resembles a theorem they learned years ago. They recognize a useful transformation, anticipate what might happen in a limiting case, or suspect that a particular symmetry will simplify the calculation.

This is what we often call intuition.

It can look almost mysterious from the outside. How did the person recognize that connection so quickly? Why did one approach seem natural while another did not?

Sometimes intuition comes from extensive experience with patterns. The person has encountered enough related problems to recognize structures that a beginner would not yet see.

Remembered knowledge provides the raw material for those connections. Practice and experience help organize it. Repeated application teaches us which relationships are useful and which are misleading.

Of course, intuition is not infallible. Experts make mistakes, and familiar patterns can lead us astray when we encounter unfamiliar situations. We still need to test our intuitions against evidence and reasoning.

But the broader point remains: knowledge that is accessible and well connected can support forms of thought that are difficult to develop when the underlying information is unfamiliar or difficult to retrieve.

Perhaps this is one way to think about expertise. It is not simply knowing more things. It is being able to access and organize what you know in ways that help you solve problems.

And that brings me back to my original frustration with memorization.

I was treating it as an end in itself, when its most important role may be what it allows us to do afterward.

## What I think about memorization now

I still think there is something deeply unsatisfying about an educational system that rewards students for reproducing information without understanding it.

Memorizing a proof without understanding its logic is not the same as learning mathematics. Reproducing a definition without being able to explain it is not the same as understanding a concept. And preparing for an examination by cramming information that disappears a week later is hardly the ideal outcome of education.

But I no longer think the alternative is to minimize memory and rely on understanding alone.

Understanding is not something that exists independently of everything we remember. It draws on concepts, examples, relationships, and experiences accumulated over time. We need access to that knowledge to connect ideas, follow arguments, and solve problems.

Nor is memory simply a passive storehouse. The act of retrieving information can strengthen learning, while repeated application can help us build more useful connections between concepts.

The distinction I find most helpful is between memorizing something to reproduce it and learning something so that it becomes available for thought.

The former can be a narrow educational exercise. The latter is one of the foundations of expertise.

I am still wary of rote learning, and I don't think everyone needs to memorize everything they encounter. But I have started to see the value in making important knowledge genuinely accessible rather than merely familiar.

When I learn something now, I want to do more than understand the explanation while it is in front of me. I want to be able to reconstruct the idea later, recognize where it applies, connect it to something else, and use it when I encounter a problem I have never seen before.

Maybe that is the distinction I was missing as a child.

We do not learn in order to remember things for an examination. We remember things, in part, so that we can continue learning long after the examination is over.

And perhaps the most useful question to ask ourselves is not whether memorization matters, but what we want our memory to make possible.

Do we want to recognize an idea when somebody explains it to us? Or do we want to carry that idea with us, ready to be used, challenged, connected, and transformed into something new?

For me, the second possibility is what makes learning interesting.

## Further reading

For anyone interested in exploring the research behind these ideas, I would start with the following papers and books.

1. **Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006).** [Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x) *Psychological Science.*

2. **Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008).** [The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408) *Science.*

3. **Dunlosky, J., et al. (2013).** [Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) *Psychological Science in the Public Interest.*

4. **Ericsson, K. A., & Kintsch, W. (1995).** [Long-Term Working Memory.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.2.211) *Psychological Review.*

5. **Brod, G., Werkle-Bergner, M., & Shing, Y. L. (2013).** [The Influence of Prior Knowledge on Memory: A Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience Perspective.](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2013.00139) *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience.*

6. **National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2018).** [How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures.](https://doi.org/10.17226/24783)

These works address different parts of the question: how retrieval improves retention, how practice affects long-term learning, how knowledge supports complex thinking, and how prior knowledge shapes the acquisition of new information. Taken together, they provide a useful starting point for thinking more carefully about what it means to learn something well.
