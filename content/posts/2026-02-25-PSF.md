Imagine trying to map the exact shape of a distant galaxy. To understand how your instrument distorts this shape, you must first know how it responds to the simplest possible input: a perfect, infinitely small point of light. In observational astrophysics, a distant star serves as a perfect empirical example of this; a point source of light, which mathematically acts as a spatial Dirac delta function, $\delta(x, y)$.

However, when you image that star, it never appears as an infinitely small point. It spreads out into a blurry spot, often surrounded by faint concentric rings.

The Point Spread Function (PSF) is the 2D irradiance distribution that results from an isolated point source. In the terminology of linear systems theory, the PSF is the impulse response of an optical system. Any extended object (like a galaxy or a nebula) can be thought of as a continuous sum of infinite point sources. Therefore, the final image you capture is the true object convolved with the system's PSF.

But the question is why does this spreading occur? The answer can be be attributed to three main culprits:

1. Diffraction: The fundamental wave nature of light. Even a mathematically perfect lens will produce a spread because the aperture truncates the wavefront.

2. Aberrations: Imperfections in the optical design or manufacturing (e.g., spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism).

3. Environmental factors: Scattering and refractive index variations in the medium (e.g., atmospheric turbulence, known as "seeing").

But for now let's strip away aberrations and environmental factors and look at a perfect optical system with a circular aperture of diameter $D$. We use the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets.

When a plane wave from a distant star passes through a circular aperture, the wavefront is truncated. The light waves diffract and interfere as they propagate to the focal plane. This is a case of Fraunhofer diffraction (far-field diffraction)

The resulting intensity distribution, $I(\theta)$, at an angle $\theta$ from the optical axis is given by the Airy pattern:$$I(\theta) = I_0 \left( \frac{2 J_1(k a \sin\theta)}{k a \sin\theta} \right)^2$$
Where:
- $I_0$ is the peak intensity at the center.
- $J_1$ is the Bessel function of the first kind of order one.- - $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ is the wavenumber.
- $a = \frac{D}{2}$ is the radius of the aperture.
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light.

The central bright spot is called the Airy disk. The first zero (the first dark ring) occurs when $k a \sin\theta = 3.8317$. Solving for $\theta$ using the small-angle approximation ($\sin\theta \approx \theta$), we get the famous Rayleigh criterion for the diffraction limit:$$\theta_{R} = 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D}$$This equation tells you the absolute maximum angular resolution a perfect telescope of diameter $D$ can achieve.

But is it this simple? Not quite. Real optical systems are not perfect. They have aberrations, and the Earth's atmosphere adds turbulence. These factors further broaden the PSF, making it more complex than the ideal Airy pattern. To model complex optical systems mathematically and computationally, we must move beyond simple wave interference and treat the optical system as a Linear, Shift-Invariant (LSI) system.

Let the true intensity distribution of the object be $O(x, y)$. Let the observed image intensity be $I(x, y)$. The relationship is defined by a 2D convolution:

$$I(x, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} O(x', y') \cdot \text{PSF}(x - x', y - y') \,dx' \,dy'$$

Or, more compactly:

$$I(x, y) = O(x, y) * \text{PSF}(x, y)$$

How do we mathematically derive the PSF for any arbitrary system? We start at the telescope's aperture, mathematically described by the Generalized Pupil Function, $\mathcal{P}(u, v)$, in the spatial frequency coordinates $(u, v)$:

$$\mathcal{P}(u, v) = A(u, v) e^{i \Phi(u, v)}$$

- $A(u, v)$ is the aperture transmission function (usually 1 inside the aperture and 0 outside).
- $\Phi(u, v)$ represents the phase errors or aberrations at the pupil. In computational physics, these are usually expanded into orthogonal basis functions called Zernike polynomials, which perfectly map to classic optical aberrations (defocus, astigmatism, coma).

The telescope lens acts as a physical Fourier transformer. The complex electric field at the focal plane, known as the Amplitude Spread Function (ASF) or coherent spread function $h(x, y)$, is the scaled 2D Fourier Transform of the generalized pupil function:$$h(x, y) = \mathcal{F} \{ \mathcal{P}(u, v) \}$$

Since detectors (like CCDs or CMOS sensors) measure intensity (power) and not the complex electric field, we take the squared magnitude to get the incoherent PSF:$$\text{PSF}(x, y) = |h(x, y)|^2 = \left| \mathcal{F} \{ \mathcal{P}(u, v) \} \right|^2$$

By the convolution theorem, convolution in the spatial domain becomes multiplication in the frequency domain. If we take the Fourier transform of the imaging equation:$$\mathcal{F}\{I\} = \mathcal{F}\{O\} \cdot \mathcal{F}\{\text{PSF}\}$$

The Fourier transform of the PSF is the Optical Transfer Function (OTF):$$\text{OTF}(f_x, f_y) = \mathcal{F}\{\text{PSF}(x, y)\}$$

By the Wiener-Khinchin theorem (or autocorrelation theorem), the OTF is also the spatial autocorrelation of the pupil function $\mathcal{P}(u, v)$.
The modulus of the OTF is the Modulation Transfer Function (MTF), which tells you how much contrast is preserved at a given spatial frequency. An aperture essentially acts as a low-pass spatial filter, cutting off high-frequency structural details (which is why resolution is limited).


However for ground-based observations, the atmosphere completely dominates the PSF. Temperature fluctuations in the atmosphere cause refractive index variations (Kolmogorov turbulence). 

The atmospheric PSF can be characterized by the Fried parameter, $r_0$. Physically, $r_0$ represents the diameter of a telescope that would have the same resolution as the atmosphere-limited seeing.

- If $D < r_0$, the system is diffraction-limited.
- If $D > r_0$, the system is seeing-limited. The PSF breaks up into rapidly changing "speckles," and a long-exposure PSF takes a Gaussian-like or Moffat-profile shape with a Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of:$$\theta_{\text{seeing}} \approx 0.98 \frac{\lambda}{r_0}$$
